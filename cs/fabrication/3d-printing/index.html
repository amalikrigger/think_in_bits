Fabrication · Lesson 1

3D Printing: Make Something Real

Print a model you find, then design and print one of your own

  • Grades 7 to 12
  • Time 4 class periods, plus open build days
  • Difficulty Beginner

Two prints, two ways of getting a 3D model. First you find a model someone else made and print it, so you learn the machine. Then you design your own in Tinkercad and print that. Keep it simple with a nameplate, or go further and design something that fixes a real problem. Either way you finish holding two objects that did not exist last week, and one of them is all yours.

What you'll learn

  • How to find a model, check it will print, and give its designer credit
  • How to design a 3D model in Tinkercad from shapes, holes and exact measurements
  • How a printer turns your model into an object layer by layer, and why resin and filament printers do it differently
  • How to run a printer properly: slice the file, set up the machine, finish the print, and leave it ready for the next person

What you need

  • A lab computer with a web browser, and Tinkercad, joined with the class code and a nickname
  • One of the lab's two printers, your pick: the Formlabs Form 3 (resin) or the MakerBot Replicator+ (filament)
  • For the MakerBot: the class flash drive
  • A ruler or calipers
  • Nitrile or neoprene gloves and safety glasses at the Formlabs wash station
You never sign in to anything. Tinkercad uses a class code and a nickname, with no account and no email. PreForm (on one lab computer) and MakerBot Print are already signed in on Mr. Krigger's account. Do not make an account on any model website.

Safety rules: resin and IPA

These are non-negotiable. Breaking one on purpose costs you printer access and earns a 0 for the day.

  • Pass the printer safety check first. Nobody touches a printer, the resin, the wash or the cure before Mr. Krigger has checked them out.
  • Gloves for anything wet: liquid resin, IPA, and any print not yet washed and cured. Nitrile or neoprene, never latex.
  • Resin on your skin: soap and water, right away. Never IPA or hand sanitizer. Then tell Mr. Krigger. Touching resin again and again can make your skin react to it for life.
  • Safety glasses for resin, IPA, and clipping supports. Supports snap and fly.
  • IPA is flammable. Bottles and the Form Wash lid stay closed. Nothing hot near it.
  • Resin waste never goes in the sink or the trash. Gloves, towels and failed parts go in the marked resin waste container.

Safety rules: every printer

  • No food or drink at the print station.
  • Hands out of any printer that is moving. The MakerBot nozzle runs at about 200°C, and its build plate stays hot after the print ends.
  • Unattended prints: a Formlabs plate may run overnight under Mr. Krigger's rule. The MakerBot never runs without an adult in the room.
  • Report spills, damage, a burn smell or an injury right away. You will never be in trouble for reporting something. You will be for hiding it.
Why the gloves are not optional. Formlabs warns that skin contact with resin can cause an allergic reaction, and once someone is sensitized it does not go away. One careless week can mean never being able to use a resin printer again.

How the printer queue works

  • Every part fits in a 40 x 40 x 25 mm box. About the size of a matchbox standing up.
  • Formlabs: one plate holds a whole class. It builds every part's layer at the same moment, so twenty short parts take about as long as one. Time depends on the tallest part. Plates run overnight.
  • MakerBot: one part at a time, under 60 minutes, only while an adult is in the room.
  • Failed prints go first next time.

Day 1

Hands on, safety, and Print 1

Day 1 start
  1. Sit at a lab computer and open a web browser.
  2. Find the class code on the board.
  3. Do not touch a printer yet. That comes after the safety check.

Goal: by the end of today you have built a nameplate in Tinkercad, passed the printer safety check, and found Print 1.

Step 1: Build a nameplate

  1. Go to tinkercad.com and click Log In. Choose Students, join your Class, type the class code, then your nickname: first name or initials, never your full name.
  2. Click Create, then 3D Design.
  3. Drag a Box onto the blue workplane. Click it, click the white squares on its sides, and type: 40 mm long, 15 mm wide, 3 mm tall.
  4. Drag Text onto the box. Type your name or a word. Make it 2 mm tall and sink it a little into the box.
  5. Drag a box around both to select them, then click Group.
Checkpoint: a grouped nameplate, 40 mm long, with raised text. Keep it. It can become your Print 2 tomorrow.

Step 2: Two printers, two ways to build

Both printers build one thin layer at a time. They do it very differently.

Step 3: The printer safety check

Before you touch a printer, Mr. Krigger checks you out at the machine, a few of you at a time. While you wait your turn, start Step 4.

  • You show where the gloves, the glasses and the resin waste container are.
  • You say what you do if resin gets on your skin.
  • You show the Form Wash lid closed, and say why IPA stays closed.
  • You show where your hands stay while the MakerBot is moving.

Miss something and you take it again. Pass it once and the printers are yours for your own projects for the rest of the year. The full rules are in the Lab Safety lesson.

Checkpoint: Mr. Krigger has checked you off.

Step 4: Find Print 1

Print 1 is a model someone else designed. Today you are learning the machine.

SiteGood for
PrintablesClean search, good filters, lots of small useful things
ThingiverseThe biggest library, plenty of classics
MakerWorldLots of new, popular models. Download the STL, not a 3MF made for another brand of printer.

Your model must: fit in 40 x 40 x 25 mm (you can shrink it), be school appropriate with no weapons or weapon parts, and download as an STL without making an account.

Write down the page link and the designer's name. It goes on your Print Card. Someone made it, and they get the credit.

Step 5: Check it fits, then save it

  1. In Tinkercad, click Import and choose the STL you downloaded.
  2. Read its size. Too big? Hold Shift and drag a corner to shrink it evenly.
  3. Make sure it sits flat on the workplane, then click Export, then .STL.
  4. Rename it like HS-JK-print1.stl.
  5. Formlabs: save it in the Plate folder on the PreForm computer. MakerBot: save it to the class flash drive. Either way, write it on the Print Queue sheet.

Your model is 30 mm wide but 60 mm tall. What do you do?

Shrink it evenly. Height sets how long a whole Formlabs plate takes, so the limit is firm. Squashing just the height turns your model into a pancake. Shift plus a corner drag keeps its shape.

Day 3 · Formlabs

Slice, print, finish

Day 3 start
  1. Using the MakerBot? Open the MakerBot tab at the top instead.
  2. Check the Print Queue sheet. If Print 1 is printed, go to Step 2 with Mr. Krigger. If not, start with Step 1.
  3. Gloves and glasses are at the wash station. You need them for Steps 2 to 5.

Goal: your prints are sliced and on a plate, Print 1 is finished and in your hand, and the printer is ready for the next person.

Step 1: Slice in PreForm

  1. PreForm is on one lab computer, already signed in. Do not sign in with your own account.
  2. Click File, then Open, and choose your STL.
  3. Check the printer says Form 3 and the material matches the cartridge in the printer.
  4. Click Orient, then Auto-Orient. Click Supports, then Auto-Generate.
  5. Look at the Printability check. Green is good. Red means it will not print: run Auto-Orient again, or tilt the part yourself.
  6. Take a screenshot of your part with its supports. It goes on your Print Card.
  7. Mr. Krigger puts the whole class on one plate and starts it.
Checkpoint: a PreForm screenshot for each print.

Step 2: Before every print

The printer has to be set up right, or the print fails and the whole plate is lost. Check these with Mr. Krigger before a plate starts.

  • Cartridge: shaken well, the right resin, pushed down level with the back of the printer, and the vent cap pressed open on top
  • Tank: the same resin as the cartridge, clicked into its rails, no cured bits floating in it, the mixer at its home on the left
  • Build platform: clean, with no old resin on it, pushed in and the handle locked down
  • Cover closed
Never pull the rubber valve off the bottom of a cartridge. It is what keeps the resin in.

Step 3: Wash and dry

Gloves and safety glasses on first. Everything here is wet with resin until the cure is done.
  1. Remove the build platform and hold it over a paper towel, parts up.
  2. Wash once in the Form Wash, lid closed, for the time on your resin's label:
Resin on the labelWash time
Grey, White, Black or Clear V55 minutes
Grey, White, Black or Clear V4 or V4.110 minutes
  1. Dry. Let the parts air dry completely when the basket lifts out.
  2. Still sticky once dry? Wash again, straight away, in clean IPA. Sticky usually means the wash IPA is old.

Step 4: Remove, cure, clip

  1. Remove the parts from the platform with the part removal tool. Push away from your other hand.
  2. Cure in the Form Cure:
Resin on the labelCure
Grey, White, Black or Clear V51 minute, no heat. Stronger: 5 minutes at 60°C.
Grey, White or Black V4 or V4.110 minutes at 60°C
Clear V4 or V4.15 to 7 minutes at 60°C
  1. Clip the supports with flush cutters, glasses on, pointing away from people. Sand the bumps if you want.
Checkpoint: Print 1 is finished and in your hand. Take a photo of it.

Step 5: Leave it ready for the next person

  1. Clean the build platform. Over a paper towel, scrape off any cured resin with the platform scraper, then wipe it with IPA on a paper towel.
  2. Close the cartridge vent cap when the printer is done for the day.
  3. Check the tank for cured bits. Found some? Tell Mr. Krigger. Only the plastic tank tool ever touches the tank. A metal tool scratches the film on the bottom and ruins the tank.
  4. A tank out of the printer goes in its case with the lid on, out of the light.
  5. Gloves, towels and support bits go in the resin waste container. Close the IPA and the Form Wash lid.
Changing the resin or the tank is done with Mr. Krigger. The resin in the tank must always match the cartridge.

Mini lesson: why a resin part grows upside down

A resin printer has a tank with a clear bottom. A laser draws one layer of your part on the bottom of the tank, and the light turns that thin film of liquid into solid plastic. The platform lifts a hair, fresh resin flows underneath, and the laser draws the next layer. Thousands of times.

Your part hangs upside down from the platform the whole time. Anything that sticks out over nothing has no layer underneath to grow from. Supports hold it there. That is also why the tank film matters so much: every layer is drawn through it, so one scratch shows up in every print after.

Day 3 · MakerBot

Slice, print, finish

Day 3 start
  1. Using the Formlabs? Open the Formlabs tab at the top instead.
  2. Check the MakerBot is running today with Mr. Krigger. If not, switch to the Formlabs tab.
  3. Check the Print Queue sheet. The MakerBot prints one part at a time, in order.

Goal: your prints are sliced and on the flash drive, Print 1 is off the plate and in your hand, and the printer is ready for the next person.

Step 1: Slice in MakerBot Print

  1. Open MakerBot Print on the lab computer. It is signed in on Mr. Krigger's UltiMaker account. Do not sign in or make an account.
  2. Check the printer is the Replicator+.
  3. Click Add Models and choose your STL from the flash drive. Click Arrange to center it, flat side down.
  4. Open Print Settings: Balanced, Raft on, Supports on if anything hangs out in the air.
  5. Click Preview. It must be under 60 minutes. Longer? Make it smaller. Take a screenshot for your Print Card.
  6. Click Export and save the .makerbot file to the flash drive, at the top level, not inside a folder.
The printer cannot read an STL. It reads the .makerbot file that MakerBot Print makes. That is the file that goes into the printer.

Step 2: Print it

  1. With Mr. Krigger there, plug the flash drive into the printer.
  2. On the printer's screen, tap Print, then USB Storage, then your .makerbot file, then Print.
  3. Watch the first few layers. The plastic should stick flat to the plate. If it curls up or drags around, tell Mr. Krigger to stop it.
  4. An adult stays in the room until it finishes.
Hands out while it moves. The nozzle runs at about 200°C and the plate stays hot after the print ends.

Step 3: Take it off the plate

  1. Wait for the screen to say it is done, then for the build plate to cool.
  2. Lift out the flexible build plate and bend it gently. The print pops loose.
  3. Peel off the raft and supports. Safety glasses on if you use pliers.
  4. Brush any plastic bits off the plate and put it back for the next person.
Checkpoint: Print 1 is in your hand. Take a photo. Filament prints are done once they are off the plate: no wash, no cure.

Step 4: Changing the filament

Out of plastic, or want a new color? Do this with Mr. Krigger.

Take the old filament out

  1. On the screen tap Filament, then Unload Filament, and wait while it heats up.
  2. When it tells you, pull the filament gently out of the extruder, then out of the tube, and take the spool off.

Put new filament in

  1. Tap Filament, then Load Filament, and wait while it heats.
  2. Put the spool on, feed the end through the tube, and push it into the extruder until it grabs it.
  3. When plastic comes out of the nozzle, it is loaded. Tap Done.
Will not go in? Cut the end at an angle with flush cutters and try again.

Mini lesson: a very precise hot glue gun

The MakerBot pulls plastic string from a spool, melts it in the nozzle, and squeezes out a line thinner than spaghetti. It draws the outline of one layer, fills the inside with a pattern, then moves up a fraction of a millimeter and draws the next layer on top.

Each layer needs something under it. That is why a part that sticks out over nothing needs supports, and why the raft goes down first: it gives your part a flat, sticky floor to start from.

Make It Fit: you measured a cable at 5.0 mm. How wide is the slot that holds it?

About 5.4 mm. Printed holes and slots come out a little small, so exactly 5.0 mm usually will not take a 5.0 mm cable. 10 mm lets it fall straight out. The fit test tells you the real answer for your printer.

AI Lab: the rules

AI is useful for design if you stay the designer.

  • Use only an AI tool on your school account that Mr. Krigger has approved. Do not make a personal account. Many AI tools need you to be 18, or 13 with a parent's permission.
  • Never type your name or anyone's personal information into it.
  • You must be able to explain every line or step it gave you. If you cannot explain it, it is not yours.

Route A · AI writes the code

OpenSCAD is a free program where you build a model by typing code. Ask an AI for OpenSCAD code with exact sizes in millimeters. Then you read it, change it, and make it right.

Route B · AI as a coach

Ask an AI to walk you through building your own idea in Tinkercad, one step at a time. You make every click and ask "why" at every step.

AI Lab, Route A: reading the code

Here is what OpenSCAD code looks like. It makes a 40 mm key tag with a hole and raised letters.

// Key tag: 40 x 20 x 3 mm, a 5 mm hole, raised letters 1 mm tall
difference() {
  cube([40, 20, 3]);
  translate([6, 10, -1]) cylinder(h = 5, d = 5, $fn = 40);
}
translate([12, 6, 3]) linear_extrude(1) text("VI", size = 8);
  1. Paste it in and press F5 to preview.
  2. Change one number yourself and press F5 again. Predict what will happen before you look.
  3. Press F6 to render, then File, Export, Export as STL.

AI Lab, Route A: can you explain it?

Quick test on the key tag code. Here are its first lines again:

difference() {
  cube([40, 20, 3]);
  translate([6, 10, -1]) cylinder(h = 5, d = 5, $fn = 40);
}

Which number do you change to make the hole bigger?

d = 5. That is the hole's diameter in millimeters. difference() takes the cylinder away from the cube, which is what makes it a hole. The 40 is the tag's length and the 3 is how thick it is.

When it goes wrong: spills and skin

Resin got on my skin.
Wash it with soap and water straight away. Never IPA or hand sanitizer. Then tell Mr. Krigger.
I spilled resin or IPA.
Step back, keep your gloves on, and tell Mr. Krigger. Do not wipe it with bare hands. Used paper towels go in the resin waste container.

When it goes wrong: models and prints

The model I downloaded is enormous, or almost invisible.
It was made in different units. In Tinkercad, read its size and scale it until the numbers are right.
MakerWorld gave me a 3MF file, not an STL.
On the model page, open the files list and download the STL. If there is only a 3MF, pick a different model.
A website wants me to sign up before I can download.
Pick a different model, or ask Mr. Krigger. Do not make an account.
My text did not print.
It was thinner than 1 mm, or floating above the base instead of sinking into it. Make it 1 mm or more and overlap it into the part.
PreForm says my part will not print (red).
Run Auto-Orient and Auto-Generate supports again. Still red? Tilt the part by hand so no big flat face points straight down.
My resin part is sticky or has a white film.
It went into the cure still wet, or the wash IPA is old. Wash it again straight away in clean IPA, let it dry fully, then cure it. Tell Mr. Krigger so he can check the IPA.
My resin part is soft or bends.
It was not cured, or not long enough. Check the cure table in the Formlabs tab for your resin. Curing a bit longer does no harm.
Two parts that should fit do not.
