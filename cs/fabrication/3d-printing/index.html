Fabrication · Lesson 1

3D Printing: Make Something Real

Print a model, then design and print something that fixes a real problem for a real person

  • Grades 7 to 12
  • Time 4 class periods, plus open build days
  • Difficulty Beginner

Somewhere in this building a cable keeps falling behind a desk, a door has no name on it, and a drawer is full of things with nowhere to go. You are going to fix one of them. First you print a model someone else made, so you learn the machine. Then you design your own part, measure it against the real thing, and print it. By the end you hold an object that did not exist last week, and someone is using it.

What you'll learn

  • How to design a 3D model in Tinkercad from shapes, holes and exact measurements
  • How a printer turns your model into an object layer by layer, and why resin and filament printers do it differently
  • How to prepare a print in the slicer and check it will work before it costs printer time
  • How to finish a resin print safely: wash, dry, cure, and clip supports
  • How to design for a real person, test it on the real problem, and improve it

What you need

  • A lab computer with a web browser, and Tinkercad, joined with the class code and a nickname
  • One of the lab's two printers: the Formlabs Form 3 (resin) or the MakerBot Replicator+ (filament). You pick.
  • A ruler or calipers, and the real object your design has to work with
  • Nitrile or neoprene gloves and safety glasses at the wash station
You never sign in to anything. Tinkercad uses a class code and a nickname, with no account and no email. The printer software on the lab computers is already set up. If a model website wants you to make an account, pick a different model.

Safety rules: resin and IPA

These are non-negotiable. Breaking one on purpose costs you printer access and earns a 0 for the day.

  • Pass the printer safety check first. Nobody touches a printer, the resin, the wash or the cure before Mr. Krigger has checked them out.
  • Gloves for anything wet: liquid resin, IPA, and any print not yet washed and cured. Nitrile or neoprene, never latex.
  • Resin on your skin: soap and water, right away. Never IPA or hand sanitizer. Then tell Mr. Krigger. Touching resin again and again can make your skin react to it for life.
  • Safety glasses for resin, IPA, and clipping supports. Supports snap and fly.
  • IPA is flammable. Bottles and the Form Wash lid stay closed. Nothing hot near it.
  • Resin waste never goes in the sink or the trash. Gloves, towels and failed parts go in the marked resin waste container.

Safety rules: every printer

  • No food or drink at the print station.
  • Hands out of any printer that is moving. The MakerBot nozzle runs at about 200°C, and its build plate stays hot after the print ends.
  • Unattended prints: a Formlabs plate may run overnight under Mr. Krigger's rule. The MakerBot never runs without an adult in the room.
  • Report spills, damage, a burn smell or an injury right away. You will never be in trouble for reporting something. You will be for hiding it.
Why the gloves are not optional. Formlabs warns that skin contact with resin can cause an allergic reaction, and once someone is sensitized it does not go away. One careless week can mean never being able to use a resin printer again.

How the printer queue works

There are two printers and a lot of you. Here is how everyone gets a print.

  • Every part fits in a 40 x 40 x 25 mm box. About the size of a matchbox standing up.
  • One plate holds a whole class. A resin printer builds every part's layer at the same moment, so twenty short parts take about as long as one. Time depends on how tall the tallest part is. That is why height is the limit that matters.
  • Formlabs plates run overnight. Get your file in the queue by the end of class and it prints that night.
  • The MakerBot prints one part at a time, under 60 minutes, only while an adult is in the room. Its queue is the same sheet, in order.
  • Failed prints go in the Reprints folder, which always goes first on the next plate.

To join the queue, save your STL into the plate folder on the PreForm computer with a name like this, then write the same name on the Print Queue sheet:

HS-JK-print1.stl

Day 1

Hands on, safety, and Print 1

Day 1 start
  1. Sit at a lab computer and open a web browser.
  2. Find the class code on the board.
  3. Do not touch the printer yet. That comes after the safety check.

Goal: by the end of today you have built something in Tinkercad, passed the printer safety check, and put Print 1 in the queue.

Step 1: Join the class and build a nameplate

  1. Go to tinkercad.com and click Log In.
  2. Choose Students, join your Class. Type the class code from the board, then your nickname: your first name or initials, never your full name.
  3. Click Create, then 3D Design.
  4. Drag a Box onto the blue workplane. Click it, then click the white squares on its sides and type exact sizes: 40 mm long, 15 mm wide, 3 mm tall.
  5. Drag Text onto the box. Type your name or a word. Make it 2 mm tall and sink it a little into the box.
  6. Drag a box around both to select them, then click Group.
Checkpoint: you have a grouped nameplate, 40 mm long, with raised text. That is a 3D model, and it took about ten minutes.

Step 2: Two printers, two ways to build

Both printers build one thin layer at a time, bottom to top. They do it very differently.

Step 3: The printer safety check

Before you touch a printer, Mr. Krigger checks you out at the machine, a few of you at a time. While you wait your turn, start Step 4.

  • You show where the gloves, the glasses and the resin waste container are.
  • You say what you do if resin gets on your skin.
  • You show the Form Wash lid closed, and say why IPA stays closed.
  • You answer one "what would you do if" question.

Miss something and you take it again. Pass it once and the printers are yours for your own projects for the rest of the year. The full rules are in the Lab Safety lesson.

Checkpoint: Mr. Krigger has checked you off on the printer safety check.

Print 1 is a model someone else designed. Today you are learning the machine, not proving you can design.

  • Fit in 40 x 40 x 25 mm. You can shrink it, so check it still looks right small.
  • Be school appropriate. No weapons or weapon parts, not even as a joke.
  • Download as an STL without making an account.

Step 5: Check it fits, then queue it

  1. In Tinkercad, click Import and choose the STL you downloaded.
  2. Click it and read its size. Too big? Hold Shift and drag a corner to shrink it evenly, or type a smaller number into one size box.
  3. Make sure it sits flat on the workplane.
  4. Click Export, then .STL.
  5. Rename the file like HS-JK-print1.stl and save it into the Plate 1 folder on the PreForm computer.
  6. Write the same name on the Print Queue sheet.

Your model is 30 mm wide but 60 mm tall. What do you do?

Shrink it evenly. Height sets how long the whole plate takes, so the limit is firm. Squashing just the height would turn your model into a pancake. Shift plus a corner drag keeps its shape.

Step 1: Pick a problem

Find something small that bugs somebody. The best problems belong to a real person you can talk to.

WhereIdeas

Step 2: Measure, then sketch with numbers

A design that is 2 mm off does not fit. Measure the real thing, not your memory of it.

  1. Measure everything your part touches. How thick is the cable? How wide is the desk edge? How big is the screw hole?
  2. Sketch your part on paper and write every measurement on the sketch.
  3. Check it fits the 40 x 40 x 25 mm box.
Checkpoint: a sketch with at least three measurements written on it.

High school: make a fit test

Before you print the whole part, print just the piece that has to fit: the slot, the hole, the clip. Make it tiny, under 10 mm tall. It prints fast and tells you whether your measurements are right before you spend a full print on them.

HS-JK-fittest.stl

You measured a cable at 5.0 mm. How wide do you make the slot that holds it?

About 5.4 mm. Printed holes and slots come out a little small, so exactly 5.0 mm usually will not take a 5.0 mm cable. 10 mm would let it fall straight out. The fit test tells you the real answer for your printer.

Finish Print 1, queue Print 2

Day 3 start
  1. Using the MakerBot? Open the MakerBot tab at the top instead.
  2. Check the Print Queue sheet. If Print 1 is printed, start at the wash station (Step 2) with Mr. Krigger. If not, start with Step 1.
  3. Gloves and glasses are at the wash station. You need them for Steps 2 and 3.

Goal: by the end of today Print 1 is finished and in your hand, and Print 2 is in the queue.

Step 1: Slice Print 2 in PreForm

  1. On the PreForm computer, open PreForm. It is already signed in. Do not sign in with your own account.
  2. Click File, then Open, and choose your Print 2 STL.
  3. Check the printer says Form 3 and the material matches the cartridge in the printer. Leave the layer height as Mr. Krigger set it.
  4. Click Orient, then Auto-Orient. Click Supports, then Auto-Generate.
  5. Look at the Printability check. Green is good. Red means it will not print: run Auto-Orient again, or tilt the part yourself.
  6. Take a screenshot of your part with its supports. It goes on your Print Card.
  7. Save your STL into the next plate's folder and write it on the sheet. Mr. Krigger lays out the whole plate and starts it.
Checkpoint: a PreForm screenshot, and Print 2 in the queue.

Step 2: Wash and dry Print 1

Gloves and safety glasses on first. Everything here is wet with resin until the cure is done.
  1. Remove the build platform from the printer and hold it over a paper towel, parts up.
  2. Wash. Put the platform in the Form Wash, close the lid, and set the time for the resin on the cartridge label:
Resin on the labelWash time
Grey, White, Black or Clear V55 minutes
Grey, White, Black or Clear V4 or V4.110 minutes
  1. Dry. When the basket lifts out, let the parts air dry completely. Wet IPA under the cure light leaves a sticky, white surface.

Step 3: Remove, cure, clip

  1. Remove the parts from the platform with the part removal tool. Push away from your other hand.
  2. Cure. Put the parts in the Form Cure, choose your resin on its screen, and use the setting it shows. For V5 standard resin that is 5 minutes with no heat. For V4 it is longer and heated, so let the screen tell you.
  3. Clip the supports with flush cutters, glasses on, pointing away from people. Sand the small bumps if you want.
  4. Clean up. Gloves, towels and support bits go in the resin waste container. Close the IPA and the Form Wash lid.
Checkpoint: Print 1 is finished and in your hand. Take a photo of it.

Mini lesson: why a resin part grows upside down

A resin printer has a tank with a clear bottom. A laser draws one layer of your part on the bottom of the tank, and the light turns that thin film of liquid into solid plastic. The platform lifts a hair, fresh resin flows underneath, and the laser draws the next layer. Thousands of times.

Your part hangs upside down from the platform the whole time. Anything that sticks out over nothing has no layer underneath to grow from. Supports are what hold it there. A part with no supports falls into the tank, and the printer keeps going anyway, drawing very careful layers onto nothing.

Step 2: Take Print 1 off the plate

Hands out until it stops. The nozzle runs at about 200°C and the plate stays hot after the print ends.
  1. Wait for the screen to say the print is done, then wait for the build plate to cool.
  2. Lift out the flexible build plate and bend it gently. The print pops loose.

Checkpoint: Print 1 is in your hand. Take a photo of it. No wash and no cure: filament prints are done once they are off the plate.

Mini lesson: a very precise hot glue gun

The MakerBot pulls plastic string from a spool, melts it in the nozzle, and squeezes out a line thinner than a strand of spaghetti. It draws the outline of one layer, fills the inside with a pattern, then moves up a fraction of a millimeter and draws the next layer on top. Like stacking frosting.

Each layer needs something under it. That is why a part that sticks out over nothing needs supports, and why the raft goes down first: it gives your part a flat, sticky floor to start from.

While you wait, and if it fails

Open build days

  • Finish your Print Card. Most people underestimate this one.
  • Version 2. If Print 2 is not perfect yet, improve it.
  • AI Lab, next. Bonus, optional.
  • Things the lab needs. Ask Mr. Krigger for the list. A part used every day in this room is a good part.

If your print failed

  1. Look at what came out, or did not, and write down what you think went wrong.
  2. Check the design rules and the Printability check, fix it, and save it in the Reprints folder. Reprints go first.
  3. Failed twice for a reason that is not your design? Your STL, your slicer screenshot and a photo of the failure count as your print. Tell Mr. Krigger.

AI Lab: optional bonus

AI is useful for design if you stay the designer. This is extra, for anyone, and never instead of your own Print 2.

  • Use only an AI tool on your school account that Mr. Krigger has approved. Do not make a personal account. Many AI tools need you to be 18, or 13 with a parent's permission.
  • Never type your name or anyone's personal information into it.
  • You must be able to explain every line or step it gave you. If you cannot explain it, it is not yours.
  • Say on your Print Card that you used AI, and what for.

Route A · AI writes the code

OpenSCAD is a free program where you build a model by typing code. Ask an AI for OpenSCAD code with exact sizes in millimeters. Then you read it, change it, and make it right.

Route B · AI as a coach

Ask an AI to walk you through building your own idea in Tinkercad, one step at a time. You make every click and ask "why" at every step. This route is for learning a move you did not know.

AI Lab, Route A: reading the code

Here is what OpenSCAD code looks like. It makes a 40 mm key tag with a hole and raised letters.

// Key tag: 40 x 20 x 3 mm, a 5 mm hole, raised letters 1 mm tall
difference() {
  cube([40, 20, 3]);
  translate([6, 10, -1]) cylinder(h = 5, d = 5, $fn = 40);
}
translate([12, 6, 3]) linear_extrude(1) text("VI", size = 8);
  1. Paste it in and press F5 to preview.
  2. Change one number yourself and press F5 again. Predict what will happen before you look.
  3. Press F6 to render, then File, Export, Export as STL.

AI Lab, Route A: can you explain it?

The rule is that you can explain every line. Quick test on the key tag code. Here are its first lines again:

difference() {
  cube([40, 20, 3]);
  translate([6, 10, -1]) cylinder(h = 5, d = 5, $fn = 40);
}

Which number do you change to make the hole bigger?

d = 5. That is the hole's diameter in millimeters. difference() takes the cylinder away from the cube, which is what makes it a hole. The 40 is the tag's length and the 3 is how thick it is.

When it goes wrong

Resin got on my skin.
Wash it with soap and water straight away. Never IPA or hand sanitizer. Then tell Mr. Krigger.
I spilled resin or IPA.
Step back, keep your gloves on, and tell Mr. Krigger. Do not wipe it with bare hands. Used paper towels go in the resin waste container.
The model I downloaded is enormous, or almost invisible.
It was made in different units. In Tinkercad, read its size and scale it until the numbers are right.
A website wants me to sign up before I can download.
Pick a different model, or ask Mr. Krigger. Do not make an account.
My text did not print.
It was thinner than 1 mm, or floating above the base instead of sinking into it. Make it 1 mm or more and overlap it into the part.
PreForm says my part will not print (red).
Run Auto-Orient and Auto-Generate supports again. Still red? Tilt the part by hand so no big flat face points straight down.
My resin part is sticky or has a white film.
It went into the cure still wet with IPA, or the wash IPA is old. Tell Mr. Krigger, and let the next part dry fully first.
My resin part is soft or bends.
It was not cured, or not long enough. Check the setting on the Form Cure screen for your resin.
Two parts that should fit do not.
Printed holes come out a little small. Make the hole 0.3 to 0.5 mm bigger. That is exactly what the fit test is for.
MakerBot: the print will not stick to the plate.
The plate may be dirty or not level, or the raft is off. Tell Mr. Krigger. Never reach in while it moves.
MakerBot: the print time is over 60 minutes.
Make the part smaller, or check you did not leave something big on the plate.
