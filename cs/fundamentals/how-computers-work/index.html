Fundamentals · Lesson 1

How Computers Work

Every machine in this room is the same machine wearing different clothes

  • Grades 7 to 12
  • Time 2 class periods
  • Difficulty Beginner

There is a 3D printer, a laser cutter, a robot, a tiny board with a screen made of lights, and a computer the size of a credit card in this room. They look nothing alike. Underneath, they are running the same four ideas, and once you can see those four ideas you stop being someone who uses machines and start being someone who understands them. That is the entire difference, and it takes about two periods.

What you'll learn

  • The four jobs every computer does: input, processing, output, storage
  • Why everything inside is binary, and what that actually means
  • The difference between memory and storage, which almost everyone gets wrong
  • What a program is and what happens when one runs
  • How to look at any machine in this lab and name its four jobs

What you need

  • A computer with a web browser
  • Your eyes and the room. Day 2 is a walk around the lab.

Day 1

The four jobs

Goal: by the end of today you can explain what a computer is doing, in order, and you know why the answer to "what is it storing" is always numbers.

Step 1: There are only four jobs

Every computer ever built, from the room-sized ones that ran the moon landing to the one in your pocket, does these four things.

JobWhat it meansOn your laptop
InputSomething comes inKeyboard, trackpad, camera, microphone
ProcessingSomething decides what to doThe CPU, doing arithmetic very fast
OutputSomething goes outScreen, speakers, a file, a print job
StorageSomething is kept for laterYour drive, full of everything you have saved

That is the whole shape. Everything else is detail, size and speed.

Why this matters here: the laser cutter is not a mysterious box. It takes a file in, works out a path, and moves a laser along it. Once you can name the four jobs, you can reason about a machine you have never touched.

Step 2: It is all numbers, and there are only two of them

Inside there are no letters, no colors and no sound. Just switches, each on (1) or off (0). One switch is a bit. Eight of them make a byte, which holds 256 patterns, enough to give every basic character its own number. Agree on which number means which letter, and switches can spell.

Checkpoint: you can read off the byte for one letter of your name.

The same trick, everywhere else

Once you accept that a number can mean a letter, the rest follows without any new ideas.

ThingWhat the number means
A letterIts agreed code. H is 72.
A colorThree numbers: how much red, green and blue
A photoMillions of those color triplets, in a grid
A soundThe height of the wave, measured 44,100 times a second
A 3D modelLists of points in space, and which points connect
A programNumbers that the CPU reads as instructions

That last row is the strange one and it is worth sitting with. The instructions are stored the same way the photos are. To the machine there is no difference. The difference is entirely in what reads them.

Step 3: Memory and storage are not the same thing

Nearly everyone mixes these up, including people who have owned computers for thirty years.

Memory (RAM)

Fast, small, and wiped the instant the power goes. It holds what you are working on right now.

This is your desk. Big enough for the things you are actively using, and everything on it hits the floor when you stand up too fast.

Storage (drive)

Slower, much bigger, and it survives being switched off. It holds everything you have saved.

This is the filing cabinet. Slower to get things out of, and still there tomorrow.

This is why unsaved work dies. Your document lives on the desk until you save it. Saving is the act of copying it into the cabinet. The power cut does not delete your work, it just ends the only place your work existed.

Step 4: Watch a program actually run

A program is a list of instructions. The CPU reads one, does it, moves to the next. It cannot skim.

Checkpoint: you can say why line 3 runs four times before giving up.

Fast, and profoundly stupid

You just watched it take eighteen steps to add four numbers together. A person does that in their head. The machine needed every single instruction spelled out, in order, with nothing assumed.

Now the other half. Your laptop runs a few billion of those steps every second. It never gets bored, never loses its place, and never decides that is close enough.

This explains two things you already met. Cracking a weak password means trying billions of guesses a second, which is only possible because each guess is a tiny stupid step. And an AI predicting the next word is the same story: a simple operation, run at a scale that stops feeling simple.

Day 2

Audit the lab

Goal: by the end of today you can walk up to any machine in this room and name its four jobs out loud.

Step 1: Same machine, different clothes

Here are two you already know, filled in for you.

MachineInputProcessingOutputStorage
Your phoneTouch, mic, cameraChip insideScreen, speakerIts drive, plus the cloud
MakerBot printerA sliced file on a USB stickBoard that reads the pathMotors, hot nozzle, an objectThe stick, and its own memory

Notice the printer's output is a physical object. That is the only unusual thing about it. Everything else is the same four jobs.

Step 2: Now you do the rest

Walk the room. For each machine, write the four jobs. Look at the actual thing rather than guessing from memory, and where you are not sure, say so and say why.

  • The Glowforge laser cutter
  • A RoboMaster
  • A micro:bit
  • A Raspberry Pi
  • One machine of your choice that is not in this room
Two are deliberately awkward. The micro:bit's storage is tiny and strange, and the RoboMaster's input includes a camera that it uses to make decisions on its own. If a machine does not fit neatly, that is the interesting part. Write down what does not fit.
Checkpoint: five machines, four jobs each, and at least one honest "I am not sure, because".

Step 3: Find the edge

Pick one machine from your list and answer one of these in a short paragraph.

Base · everyone

What would happen if this machine lost one of its four jobs? Pick a job, remove it, and describe what the machine becomes.

Stretch · required for HS, bonus for MS

Find something in this room that stores information without being a computer at all. Explain what it stores, how it survives losing power, and why it is not a computer.

Vocabulary

TermWhat it means
BitOne switch, on or off, written as 1 or 0. The smallest thing a computer holds.
ByteEight bits together, which is enough for one basic character
BinaryCounting with only two digits, because a switch has only two positions
CPUThe part that reads instructions and does them, one at a time, extremely fast
RAMWorking memory. Fast, small, and gone the moment the power is
StorageThe drive. Slower, far bigger, and still there tomorrow
ProgramA list of instructions stored as numbers, like everything else
LoopInstructions that send the CPU back to repeat a section
InputAnything coming in: a key, a tap, a sensor reading, a file
OutputAnything going out: pixels, sound, a saved file, a printed object

When it goes wrong

The bits converter shows more than 8 digits for one character.
You typed something outside the basic set, like an emoji or an accented letter. Those need more than one byte, which is exactly why they were a mess to get working on the early internet.
I stepped the program and nothing changed.
Some lines only check a condition and decide where to go next, so memory stays the same. That counts as a step. The CPU still had to do it.
Why does it go back to line 2 instead of just repeating?
Because the condition has to be checked every single time. If it did not check, it would loop forever, which is a bug you will write for real at some point.
I cannot work out what the laser cutter stores.
Good, that is the awkward one. Ask what it has to remember while a job is running, and whether it remembers anything after the job finishes. Write down what you conclude and why.
Is a calculator a computer?
Run it through the four jobs and see. It takes input, processes, and outputs. The interesting question is storage, and whether it can follow a list of instructions you wrote. That question is most of what separates a calculator from a computer.
