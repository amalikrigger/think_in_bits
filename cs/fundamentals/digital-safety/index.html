Fundamentals · Lesson 1

Digital Safety and Secrets

Become a hard target, then learn how to actually keep a secret

  • Grades 7 to 12
  • Time 2 class periods
  • Difficulty Beginner

You already own things worth stealing. Not your phone. Your accounts, which someone can resell in bulk without ever meeting you. Over two days you will make your passwords genuinely expensive to crack, switch on the one setting that stops most break-ins cold, learn to spot a fake message that has perfect spelling, and find out how programmers keep secrets out of code they put on the internet. That last part is why you will not accidentally publish a password in every project you build this year.

What you'll learn

  • How to build a passphrase that would take centuries to crack
  • How two-factor authentication stops someone who already has your password
  • How to spot phishing even when the grammar is perfect and the sender looks real
  • What a secret is in programming, and why it never goes in your code
  • How a .env file keeps a key out of the file you share

What you need

  • A computer with a web browser
  • No phone in class. Two parts of this lesson work best with your own phone and accounts, so you do them at home. Each one also has an in-class option, so nobody is stuck.
  • For the home parts: one account you actually control and can change settings on. A personal account is fine and is usually easier than a school one.
  • Only for the optional Day 2 bonus: VS Code. It is on the lab machines. No VS Code at home? Skip the bonus. Nothing else in this lesson needs it.

Step 1: Watch a bad password die

Every year, security researchers publish the most common passwords found in leaked databases. Every year, it is basically this list. People are wonderfully predictable.

RankPasswordTime to crack
1123456under a second

3qwertyunder a second
4abc123under a second
5Your birthdayminutes to hours

Nobody is sitting at a keyboard typing guesses at your account. They run software against a stolen list at millions of tries per second. Anything on that table fell over before you finished reading this sentence.

Test one yourself

Never type a password you actually use, here or anywhere else. Type something similar. This page sends nothing anywhere, but that is a promise you cannot verify on someone else's site, so build the habit now.

Try password1, then your pet's name and a year, then a whole sentence. Then mash your keyboard for forty characters. It holds up, and you will never remember it. Keep that thought. There is a well known public version of this at security.org, worth bookmarking for the day you set up something that matters. Same warning applies there.

Checkpoint: you have made it say Instant and you have made it say Centuries.

Step 2: Build a passphrase

Length beats cleverness. Every character you add multiplies the work, and multiplying gets out of hand fast, which is the one time that works in your favor.

LengthTime to crack by brute force
6 charactersseconds
8 charactershours
12 characterscenturies
16+ characterseffectively impossible

Four or five unrelated words does it. Unrelated is the load-bearing word here, because saint-croix-high-school is four words and still weak. Anyone who knows you would guess it.

Do this: generate three, test each in the meter above, and write down the crack time. Those three go in your submission.

Checkpoint: three passphrases written down, each showing centuries or longer.

Why length beats symbols

Schools and websites spent twenty years demanding a capital, a number, and a symbol. What they got was Password1!, roughly two hundred million times. Everyone follows the rule the same lazy way, so attackers just try the lazy way first.

Picture a combination lock. Symbols give you more numbers on each dial. Length gives you more dials. Adding a dial multiplies everything that came before it, which is why length wins and it is not close.

Four random words give you enough dials that no computer on Earth gets through in your lifetime, or your grandchildren's. A long phrase you can actually remember beats a short scramble you end up writing on a sticky note under your keyboard, which is where roughly half of all strong passwords go to die.

Where do you keep all of these?

Three strong passphrases is easy. Forty accounts is not. Nobody can memorize forty, so people reuse one password everywhere. Then one leaked website opens every account at once, because attackers try each stolen password on every other site. That has a name, credential stuffing, and it is why reuse is the worst habit on this table.

WhereVerdictWhy
In your head, for 2 or 3 accountsGoodYour email and your password manager. The ones that unlock everything else
A password managerBest for the restOne strong passphrase unlocks a vault that makes and remembers a different password for every site
Paper, in a drawer at homeFineNot on your monitor. A burglar is a much smaller risk than a hacker
A note on your phone called “passwords”BadAnyone who gets into that account or that phone gets everything, in plain text
The same password everywhereWorstOne leak, every account
On a school or lab computer, never save a password. When the browser offers, click Never or the X. It is not your computer, and the next person who sits down can use whatever it saved. Log out when you finish.

What a password manager actually does

My vault, unlocked with one master passphrase3 saved

  • It makes the passwords. Long, random, different on every site. You never see most of them and you never need to.
  • It only fills the real site. A fake site with a number one in the name does not match anything saved, so nothing fills in. That is your phishing alarm, for free.

Not required, not graded, and not for school computers. If you want one, set it up at home with a parent or guardian. The easy start needs nothing new: the password manager already built into Chrome, Edge, Safari or Firefox, or Apple Passwords. Bitwarden is a free dedicated one. Check the age rule before you sign up for anything. Bitwarden and Google accounts both require you to be 13 or older (checked October 2026).

Step 3: Make a stolen password useless

Two-factor means logging in takes two things: something you know (your passphrase) and something you have (your phone, or an app, or a key). Someone who steals your password still cannot get in.

MethodSecurityHow it works
Text message codeGoodA code is texted to your phone
Authenticator appBetterAn app generates a code that changes every 30 seconds
Hardware keyBestA physical device you plug in
Fingerprint or faceGoodYour device confirms it is you

Text codes are the weakest of the four because of SIM swapping, which you will read about in a moment. They are still far better than nothing.

Turn it on somewhere that matters. Email first if you can, because password resets for everything else land there.

Step 3b: Turn it on, at home

Phones stay away in class. Turning on two-factor needs your own phone and your own account, so do the real thing at home tonight. Short on time or no phone at home? The next slide has an in-class version.

At home · the real thing

  1. Open your email account's security settings.
  2. Find two-factor authentication. The name changes by site, so look for “2-step verification” or “two-factor”.
  3. Turn it on.
  4. Screenshot the confirmation with anything private blurred.
Checkpoint: a screenshot showing two-factor is on, private details blurred.

Step 3c: The in-class version

No phone needed. If you did the home version, you can skip this and keep going. Tap an answer to see why.

A stranger has your password. What stops them getting in?

Two-factor. A stolen password is only the first of two things they need. The second one is on your phone, an app, or a key in your pocket, and they do not have it.

Step 3c: Question 2

Which of these is the weakest way to get a code?

A text message. Someone can take over your phone number with a SIM swap, and then the codes come to them. It is still far better than no second factor at all.
Checkpoint: both questions answered.

Step 4: Spot the phish

Phishing is someone pretending to be a company or a person you trust so that you hand something over. This is how people actually get hacked. Nobody is in a dark room breaking your encryption with a progress bar. They just asked, politely, while wearing a costume.

Terrible spelling used to give these away for free. AI ended that overnight. So stop judging the writing and start checking the structure.

  • Read the sender address itself. The display name is free to type. The name says Apple Support and the address says support@apple-security-update-2026.com.
  • Urgency and threats. "Your account will be DELETED in 24 hours." Real companies spend a fortune trying to keep you. They are not deleting you on a Tuesday over an unclicked link.
  • A generic greeting. "Dear Customer" from a company that knows your name.
  • The link does not match. Hover first. paypa1.com uses a number one. paypal.security-update.com belongs to security-update.com. The real domain is the part right before the first single slash.
  • An attachment you did not ask for. Especially .exe, .zip, and .scr.
  • Too good to be true. Reader, it is not true.

Day 2

Protect your data and your code

Day 2 start
  1. Nothing to set up for the main lesson. Photos, voices and the secrets rule are all on this page.
  2. The VS Code part is a bonus. Only open VS Code if you want it. If you already made secret-test last time, use File → Open Recent to get back to it.

Goal: by the end of today you know what you are giving away without meaning to, and you know where a password belongs in a project you are going to share.

Step 1: What your photos tell strangers

Here is what someone builds from pieces that each feel harmless.

What they getWhat they do with it
Full name and birthdayOpen accounts in your name
Email and passwordGet into your other accounts, then message your friends as you
Phone numberSIM swap, taking over your number and every code sent to it

Check the background of a photo for a house number, a street sign, a school logo, a license plate, a package label, or a screen with a name on it. You can practice two ways. In class, use the photo on the next slide. At home, look through the last five photos you posted or sent, without opening anything private.

The rules that follow are short. Keep profiles private. Post the vacation photos after you get home. Check the background before you post. Ask whether you would be fine with a stranger, a teacher, and an employer all seeing it.

Checkpoint: you found at least one identifying detail in the background of a photo, the class photo or one of your own.

Step 1b: Spot the details

Jordan posted this photo today with the caption “new hoodie!” Click everything in it that tells a stranger something about Jordan. Not everything counts.

Step 2: Assume the voice can be faked

A few seconds of audio is enough to clone a voice. Video of a real person saying things they never said is cheap now. A profile photo of a person who has never existed takes one click.

You cannot win this by squinting harder. The fakes improve every year and your eyes have not had an upgrade since you were born. So the defense has to be a habit rather than a skill.

Verify through a different channel. A call from a panicked family member asking for money gets hung up on and called back on the number you already have. A message from a friend asking for a code gets a text to that friend. A news claim gets checked against a second source you went and found yourself.

This one habit covers voice cloning, deepfake video, hacked accounts, and every scam that has not been invented yet, because it never depends on you detecting the fake.

Step 3: What actually counts as a secret

A secret is any value that proves who you are to a computer. A password. An API key. A Wi-Fi password. A database login. A token an app uses to talk to a service.

Here is the problem, and it is the reason this sits in a Computer Science class instead of a health class.

# BAD. Never do this.
api_key = "sk-a83ff20c9d4e1b77c5"

response = ask_the_ai(api_key, "explain recursion")

That key now lives inside your code, and it travels everywhere the code travels. Post the project to GitHub and the key is public. Share the folder with a partner and they have your key. Submit a screenshot and the key is in the picture. Bots scan public code for keys around the clock, and people have woken up to four figure bills from a key they published before dinner.

Putting a key in your code is like taping your house key to your front door, then posting a photo of your door, then being surprised.

The rule: the code goes in the file you share. The secret goes in a file you never share.

Step 4: Put the secret somewhere safe

A .env file holds the secrets. It sits next to your code and it never leaves your machine.

Base · everyone

app.py

import os
from dotenv import load_dotenv

load_dotenv()
api_key = os.getenv("API_KEY")

.env

API_KEY=sk-a83ff20c9d4e1b77c5

So which one is safe to post?

app.py only. It never contains the key, just the name API_KEY. Keeping the code shareable while the secret stays behind is the entire point of the pattern. .env holds the real value and never leaves your machine.

Optional bonus: build it yourself

This part is optional, for high school and middle school. It is a bonus, not a requirement. You need VS Code for it, so if you do not have VS Code, skip ahead to “Before you leave today”. You lose nothing by skipping it.

If you have never opened a terminal before, that is fine. This is written for exactly that.

What a terminal is, in one sentence. A window where you type commands instead of clicking things. That is the whole idea. You are going to type two commands in it.

We do all of this inside VS Code, which is already on the lab machines, because VS Code lets you make files whose names start with a dot. Finder and File Explorer both fight you on that, and it is the single most annoying part of this for beginners.

  2. Go to File → Open Folder

Step B: Make three files

For each one, hover the sidebar and click the New File icon, then type the name exactly, including the dot at the front where there is one. Then click Copy on the box below, click inside the file, and paste.

.env   (the secret. Note the dot.)

API_KEY=not-a-real-key-12345

.gitignore   (the part everyone forgets. Also a dot.)

.env

read_secret.py   (the code. No dot at the front.)

import os
from dotenv import load_dotenv

load_dotenv()

Checkpoint: three files in the sidebar. Save each one with Cmd+S or Ctrl+S. A white dot on the tab means unsaved.

Step C: Open the terminal

In VS Code, go to Terminal → New Terminal. A panel opens at the bottom.

It already starts inside your secret-test folder, which is the reason we are doing it this way. You never have to learn how to navigate folders from a command line today.

Check Python is there. Click Copy, click inside the terminal, paste (Ctrl+V, or Cmd+V on a Mac), then tap the Enter key:

python3 --version

You want something like Python 3.12.1. Any 3 is fine.

If it says command not found, try python --version instead, and use python in place of python3 for the rest of this. If neither works, put your hand up.
Checkpoint: the terminal printed a Python version number.

Step D: Install the one library you need

Your code says from dotenv import load_dotenv, which is you borrowing somebody else's code. It has to be on this machine first.

pip3 install python-dotenv

You will see several lines scroll past. The one you want at the end is:

Successfully installed python-dotenv-1.x.x
Already satisfied instead means somebody installed it on this machine before you. That counts. Carry on.
If pip3 is not found, try pip install python-dotenv, then python3 -m pip install python-dotenv. One of the three works.
Checkpoint: the terminal said Successfully installed, or already satisfied.

Step E: Run it

python3 read_secret.py

What you are looking for:

Loaded key: not-a-real-key-12345

Now look back at read_secret.py. The key is printed on your screen, and the key is nowhere in that file. That is the entire point, and you just built it.

Checkpoint: your terminal printed the key. Screenshot the terminal, and screenshot your .gitignore. Those two are your optional bonus hand-in.

If it did not work

Every one of these is normal and every one of them has a two second fix.

What you sawWhat it means
Loaded key: NoneThe file is not being found or read. Usually the name is .env.txt, or there are spaces around the =. It must read API_KEY=value exactly.
ModuleNotFoundError: No module named 'dotenv'Step D did not finish. Run the install again and read the last line.
can't open fileThe terminal is not in your folder, or the filename is spelled differently. Check the sidebar for the exact name.
Nothing happens at allYou probably did not tap the Enter key, or the file was never saved. Look for a white dot on the tab.
Getting an error is not failing. Reading the last line of an error and acting on it is the actual skill. You will do this for the rest of your life in this subject.

Watch one line save you $400

Same project, pushed twice. The only difference is whether .gitignore exists.

Checkpoint: you have run it both ways and seen the key appear in one and not the other.

Why .gitignore is the part people forget

load_dotenv() reads the .env file and hands the values to your program at the moment it runs. Your code asks for API_KEY by name and never contains the value.

.gitignore is the second half, and skipping it is the single most common way keys leak. It is a list of files Git refuses to include when you save or upload your work. Without that one line, the moment you push your project the .env goes with it, and everything you just did was for nothing.

Real companies leak real keys this exact way, constantly, with entire security teams on payroll. This is not a beginner mistake. It is the mistake.

When it goes wrong

My school account will not let me turn on two-factor.
It is school managed and locked down, which is IT doing its job. Use a personal account instead. Anything you actually control counts.
I do not want to screenshot my real account.
Blur everything except the confirmation that two-factor is on. That is all that is being checked.
The checker says my passphrase is weak.
Your words are related to each other, or to you, or to something on your desk right now. The meter can tell. Pick words that have no business being in the same sentence.
pip install python-dotenv says pip is not found.
Try pip3 install python-dotenv, or python3 -m pip install python-dotenv.
My script prints "Loaded key: None".
Three suspects, in order of guilt. The file got saved as .env.txt instead of .env, the file is in a different folder from the script, or you put spaces around the =. It has to read API_KEY=value with nothing breathing in between.
I cannot see the .env file in my folder.
Anything starting with a dot is hidden, which is the whole idea. On Mac press Cmd Shift . in Finder. On Windows turn on hidden items in the View tab.
I do not have anything worth stealing.
Your accounts sell in bulk, and your identity is worth more than your accounts. Your phone number by itself can reset logins you have completely forgotten you own. You are not the target. You are inventory, and inventory is easier.
