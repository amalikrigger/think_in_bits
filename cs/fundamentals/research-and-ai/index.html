Fundamentals · Lesson 2

Research and AI

Find real answers, catch the fake ones, and use AI without letting it think for you

  • Grades 7 to 12
  • Time 2 class periods
  • Difficulty Beginner

The internet holds the answer to almost anything you will ever need to know. It also holds a confident, well written, completely wrong answer to the same question. Over two days you will learn to search like someone who knows what they are doing, check whether a source deserves your trust, and use AI as a tool that makes you smarter instead of one that does your thinking and leaves you unable to explain your own work.

What you'll learn

  • How to search with operators instead of typing whole questions
  • How to run the SIFT check on a source before you trust it
  • How to tell a primary source from someone's summary of one
  • How to use AI to understand something, and where that crosses into cheating
  • How to catch a hallucination, which is AI stating something false with total confidence

What you need

  • A computer with a web browser
  • An AI tool you are old enough to use. Check the age table on Day 2 before you pick one.
  • Something you are actually stuck on from another class, for the Day 2 activity

Day 1

Find it and check it

Goal: by the end of today you can find a specific fact fast, and you can say out loud why you do or do not trust the page you found it on.

Step 1: Stop typing questions

Most people type a whole sentence into the search bar.

What is the best programming language to learn to get a job in 2026?

That works, and it is slow. Search engines throw away most of those words anyway. Here are five operators that do the actual work.

OperatorWhat you typeWhat it does
Keywordsbest programming language jobs 2026Says the same thing with nothing wasted

filetype:raspberry pi projects filetype:pdfFinds one kind of file
site: is the one that changes how you work. It lets you search a source you already trust instead of searching the whole internet and hoping.

Step 2: The Search Challenge

Three questions. For each one, write down the exact query you typed and the source you landed on. The query matters as much as the answer.

  1. Find a beginner Raspberry Pi project guide as a PDF. Use filetype:.
  2. Find what the National Hurricane Center itself published about a storm that affected St. Croix. Use site:nhc.noaa.gov.
  3. Find the salary range for a job you would actually want, in the Virgin Islands. Use quotes around the exact phrase.

Question 3 is harder than it looks. Territory specific salary data is thin, and you may not find a clean number.

If you cannot find it, write "I could not find this" and say where you looked. That is a real research result. Inventing a number, or grabbing a US national average and calling it a Virgin Islands number, is how bad information gets made.

Step 3: Run SIFT before you trust it

Four moves, in order, before you believe anything.

MoveWhat you actually do
SStopDo not share it yet. Do not screenshot it yet. Pause.
IInvestigate the sourceWho wrote this. What is this site. Are they selling something.
FFind better coverageSearch the same claim. Does anyone credible say it too.
TTrace to the originalArticles cite studies. Go read the study.
T is the one people skip, and it is where the errors hide. An article says "a study found teenagers who code earn more." You trace it, and the study surveyed 200 adults in one city. The claim was not wrong exactly, it was just much smaller than the headline made it sound.

What a weak source looks like

  • No author. "Staff Writer", or nothing at all.
  • No date. You cannot tell if this is from last month or 2014.
  • Emotional headline. "SHOCKING", "You Won't Believe".
  • Ads outnumber content. A low effort site making money on clicks.
  • Nothing cited. Big claims, no links to evidence.
  • Only one site says it. Nobody credible repeated it.

How much a source is worth

TierWhat it isExamples
BestThe original thingPublished studies, government data, official documentation
GoodEstablished outlets and schoolsAP, Reuters, BBC, .edu sites, MDN Web Docs
Okay to startCommunity checkedStack Overflow answers with high votes, Wikipedia, then follow its sources
CarefulOne person's takeBlog posts, videos, social media
AvoidMade for clicksNo author, filler articles, sites that exist to rank on search

Find one source you trust and one you do not, on any tech topic. Run SIFT on both. Screenshot both and write which SIFT move gave the bad one away.

Checkpoint: two sources, two verdicts, and you can name the move that exposed the weak one.

Step 2: Know where the line is

Same assignment, two ways of using the same tool.

This is cheating

Write my essay about the American Revolution.

You learned nothing, you submitted work that is not yours, and you cannot answer a single follow up question about it.

This is learning

I am writing an essay about the American Revolution. Explain the main
causes simply so I understand them before I write anything.
The test is one question: am I using this to understand, or to avoid understanding?

You may use AI to explain things, to help you debug, and to write code when Mr. Krigger approves it or the assignment asks for it. What you submit has to be yours, and you have to be able to explain any part of it on demand.

Step 3: Prompt patterns worth keeping

Try at least two of these on something you are stuck on right now, from any class.

Explain it at my level

Explain how CSS Flexbox works to an 8th grader. Use one real example.

Quiz me, and do not give it away

Give me 5 practice questions on Python loops. Do not show any answers
until I have tried each one.

Check my wrong idea

I think a for loop in Python always runs exactly 10 times. Am I right?
If not, explain why.

Explain why, not just what

Walk me through setting up a Raspberry Pi web server. For every step,
explain why the step is there.
Checkpoint: two patterns run on something real, and you understand something you did not understand at the start of the period.

Why a confident answer can still be invented

An AI does not look things up and report back. It predicts what text should come next, one piece at a time, based on patterns in an enormous amount of writing it was trained on.

That is why it writes so well and why it can be so wrong. Fluent writing and true writing are produced by the same machinery, so it has no internal signal telling it which one it just did. When it does not know something, it does not stop. It produces the most plausible looking text, which is exactly what a correct answer would also look like.

This is called a hallucination. It will cite studies that were never published, quote people who never said it, and give you a street address for a business that does not exist, all in the same confident tone it uses for things that are true.

Three other limits. It has a knowledge cutoff, so it can be confidently out of date. It reflects the bias in what it was trained on. And if you let it answer everything for you, your own ability quietly stops developing, which is the slowest and most expensive problem on this list.

The fix for all four is the same, and you already learned it yesterday. Trace it to the original.

Step 4: Catch one

Base · everyone

Ask your AI tool something specific and local, where you can check the answer yourself:

  • The address or opening hours of a specific business on St. Croix
  • Who currently holds a specific local office
  • What a particular store charges for something

Then verify it against the real source. Write down what it said, what is actually true, and how you checked.

Stretch · required for HS, bonus for MS

Make it cite something, then chase the citation.

  1. Ask a factual question and add: Cite your sources with links.
  2. Take every citation it gives you and try to open it
  3. For each one, record: does the page exist, does it say what the AI claimed, is it a real source
The most dangerous case is a link that works but does not contain the claim. It looks verified until you actually read it.
Checkpoint: one documented case of the AI being wrong, out of date, or citing something that does not support its claim. If everything checked out, say so and show your checking.

Vocabulary

TermWhat it means
OperatorA search command like site: or filetype: that narrows results instead of adding words
SIFTStop, Investigate, Find better coverage, Trace to the original
Primary SourceThe original: the study itself, the official data, the person who was there
Secondary SourceSomebody's account of a primary source, like a news article about a study
HallucinationAI stating something false in the same confident tone it uses for true things
Knowledge CutoffThe date an AI's training data stops, after which it does not know what happened
BiasA slant an AI inherits from the writing it was trained on
PromptWhat you type to an AI tool
Content FarmA site that publishes low effort articles to collect ad clicks
PaywallA page that requires payment to read, which is a real research obstacle and not a reason to use a worse source

Stuck?

My site: search returns nothing.
Check the domain is spelled exactly right and drop the https:// and www.. It should read site:nhc.noaa.gov, with no space after the colon.
I cannot find the Virgin Islands salary number.
Good. That is the real answer for a lot of territory specific data. Write down where you looked and say you could not find it.
The AI gave me a source but the link is dead.
Write that down, it is exactly what the assignment is looking for. Then search the title separately to see whether the source ever existed.
I am under 18, so what do I use?
Microsoft Copilot. The school supplies it and the minimum age is 13. Ask Mr. Krigger if you cannot get into it.
The article I need is behind a paywall.
Check whether your library gives you access, look for the original study the article is describing, or note the paywall and use a different source. Do not pay for anything for this class.
Everything the AI told me checked out.
Then say so and show your checking. Being unable to find a mistake is a legitimate result, as long as you actually looked.
