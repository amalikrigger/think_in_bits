Do Now · 5 min

Solve the pair.

\(x + y = 5\)
\(x^2 + y^2 = 17\)
rearrange the linear  \(y = 5 - x\)
put it into the quadratic  \(x^2 + (5 - x)^2 = 17\)
expand and collect  \(2x^2 - 10x + 8 = 0\)
divide both sides by 2  \(x^2 - 5x + 4 = 0\)
\(x = 1\) or \(x = 4\), so the answers are \((1, 4)\) and \((4, 1)\)

That last quadratic, \(x^2 - 5x + 4 = 0\), is the same one that ran through Session 2.

The Intersection Lab · 4 min

Two answers, because there are two crossing points.

\(x + y = 5\) is a straight line. \(x^2 + y^2 = 17\) is a circle. Solving them together asks one question: where do they touch? Drag the slider to slide the line across the circle.

The discriminant is back. \(\Delta > 0\) means the line cuts the circle twice. \(\Delta = 0\) means it just touches, which is the tangency you met in Session 1. \(\Delta < 0\) means it misses, and the algebra says the same thing by refusing to give you a real answer.

Direct instruction · 1 of 6

One linear + one quadratic: substitute, solve, pair up.

  1. Substitute the linear equation into the quadratic, so only one letter is left.
  2. Solve the quadratic you get. Usually two \(x\)-values.
  3. Back-substitute each one. Every \(x\) needs its partner \(y\). The answer is a pair of coordinates: an \(x\)-value and its matching \(y\)-value.

Stopping at "\(x = 1\) or \(x = 4\)" is half an answer. Those are just the two places on the picture, read off the \(x\)-axis. You still owe the height of each one: \(y = 5 - 1 = 4\), and \(y = 5 - 4 = 1\). The answer is \((1, 4)\) and \((4, 1)\).

Why not use elimination, the way you would with two linear equations? Elimination scales one or both equations so a variable's coefficient matches the other equation, then adds or subtracts to cancel that variable out.

try it: multiply the linear equation by 3  \(3 \times (x + y = 5) \to 3x + 3y = 15\): still only \(x\) and \(y\) to the first power, nothing to cancel against \(x^2 + y^2 = 17\)

Multiplying the linear equation by any constant keeps it linear. It never grows an \(x^2\) or a \(y^2\) to cancel, so no multiple of it lines up with the quadratic. Substitution is the only route, which is the word the syllabus uses.

Direct instruction · 2 of 6 · worked

All of it, on one pair.

linear  \(2x + y = 7\)   quadratic  \(x^2 + xy = 10\)
1 · rearrange the linear for \(y\)  \(y = 7 - 2x\)
2 · substitute into the quadratic  \(x^2 + x(7 - 2x) = 10\)
3 · expand the bracket  \(x^2 + 7x - 2x^2 = 10\)
4 · everything on one side  \(-x^2 + 7x - 10 = 0\)
5 · multiply by \(-1\) so \(x^2\) is positive  \(x^2 - 7x + 10 = 0\)
6 · factor and solve  \((x - 2)(x - 5) = 0 \to x = 2\) or \(x = 5\)
7 · each \(x\) back into \(y = 7 - 2x\)  \(x = 2 \to y = 3\)  ·  \(x = 5 \to y = -3\)
\((2, 3)\) and \((5, -3)\)
check both in both originals  \(2(2) + 3 = 7\) ✓, \(2^2 + 2(3) = 10\) ✓  ·  \(2(5) + (-3) = 7\) ✓, \(5^2 + 5(-3) = 10\) ✓

Why each \(x\) keeps its own \(y\): the \(y\) came out of that \(x\) through \(y = 7 - 2x\), so they travel together. Swap them and it breaks. Try \((2, -3)\): \(2(2) + (-3) = 1\), and the linear equation wanted 7.

Direct instruction · 3 of 6

The nickname trick.

You already know how to solve anything shaped like \(u^2 + bu + c = 0\). So when an equation is too ugly to solve, the move is to make it that shape.

Find the piece that keeps showing up. Cover it with a single letter, \(u\). Solve the easy equation you are left with. Then uncover it and finish the job.

A nickname is not an answer. If a friend is called Red, "Red" is not a person: you still have to go find the person. Same here. Landing on \(u = 4\) does not end the question, it starts the last step.

Direct instruction · 4 of 6

Spot the repeated piece.

In each one, the same thing appears twice: once squared, once on its own. That repeated thing is what gets the nickname.

\(x^4 - 5x^2 + 4 = 0\) (\(x^4\) is \((x^2)^2\), so let \(u = x^2\))
\(x - 4\sqrt{x} + 3 = 0\) (\(x\) is \((\sqrt{x})^2\), so let \(u = \sqrt{x}\))
\(\tan^2 x - \tan x - 1 = 0\) (let \(u = \tan x\))

Standard form first, every time. That third one is usually printed as \(\tan^2 x = 1 + \tan x\), and nothing works until everything is on one side.

The part practice sets usually skip is what comes after: swapping back to \(x\) once \(u\) is known. Getting "\(u = 1\)" is not the finish line, it is one more equation to solve.

Direct instruction · 5 of 6

The full arc, once.

\(x^4 - 5x^2 + 4 = 0\)
let \(u = x^2\)  \(u^2 - 5u + 4 = 0 \to (u - 1)(u - 4) = 0 \to u = 1\) or \(4\)
undo the nickname  \(x^2 = 1 \to x = \pm 1\)  ·  \(x^2 = 4 \to x = \pm 2\)
Four roots: \(x = -2, -1, 1, 2\)

Two \(u\)-values turned into four \(x\)-values, because each \(x^2 = \text{number}\) question has a positive and a negative answer. Dropping the \(\pm\) is how a correct method still loses half the marks.

This one had four real roots. A degree-four equation does not always get four. You will see one in a moment that gets two.

Direct instruction · 6 of 6

Some answers for \(u\) are impossible.

Before you undo the nickname, ask what that nickname is allowed to be, and why.

\(u = x^2\) cannot be negative, since squaring a real number never gives a negative result
\(u = \sqrt{x}\) cannot be negative either, since the \(\sqrt{\ }\) symbol always means the non-negative root
\(u = \cos x\) lives between \(-1\) and \(1\), the whole range cosine ever takes
\(u = x^3\) can be anything, negatives included, since every real number has exactly one real cube root

Check the nickname you actually used. Do not assume negative means reject: it depends which of the four you're using.

A \(u\)-value outside its range has no solution. Write down why you're rejecting it: that line is what earns the mark. The safety check is always the same, put your answer back into the original equation and see if it holds.

Try it: pick a nickname, pick a value, and watch what comes back

Worked example · 7 min · wkshtF Q1

\(x^4 - 3x^2 - 4 = 0\)

let \(u = x^2\)  \(u^2 - 3u - 4 = 0 \to (u - 4)(u + 1) = 0 \to u = 4\) or \(u = -1\)
check each \(u\) against its range  \(u = x^2\), so \(u\) cannot be negative
\(x^2 = 4 \to x = \pm 2\)  ·  \(x^2 = -1 \to\) no real solution, reject
\(x = \pm 2\) only
check  \(x = 2\): \(16 - 12 - 4 = 0\) ✓  ·  \(x = -2\): \(16 - 12 - 4 = 0\) ✓

Same \(u = x^2\) substitution as the arc before it, and this time one branch really does get rejected. Writing down why \(u = -1\) is rejected earns the mark. An answer of \(x = \pm 2\) with no reason given looks like a guess.

Q5  \(x^4 = 10x^2 - 9\)
standard form first: everything on one side, equals zero  \(x^4 - 10x^2 + 9 = 0\)
\(u = x^2\)  \(u^2 - 10u + 9 = 0 \to (u - 1)(u - 9) = 0 \to u = 1\) or \(9\)
both are \(\ge 0\), so keep both: \(x^2 = 1 \to x = \pm 1\) · \(x^2 = 9 \to x = \pm 3\)

Q4  \(3\sin^2 x + 2\sin x - 1 = 0\)
\(u = \sin x\)  \(3u^2 + 2u - 1 = 0 \to (3u - 1)(u + 1) = 0 \to u = \dfrac{1}{3}\) or \(-1\)
both sit inside \(-1 \le \sin x \le 1\), so both survive

check  \(3\left(\dfrac{1}{3}\right)^2 + 2\left(\dfrac{1}{3}\right) - 1 = \dfrac{1}{3} + \dfrac{2}{3} - 1 = 0\) ✓  ·  \(3(-1)^2 + 2(-1) - 1 = 3 - 2 - 1 = 0\) ✓
Q7  \(\cos^2 x - 2\cos x = 8\)
standard form  \(\cos^2 x - 2\cos x - 8 = 0\)
\(u = \cos x\)  \(u^2 - 2u - 8 = 0 \to (u - 4)(u + 2) = 0 \to u = 4\) or \(-2\)
both fall outside \(-1 \le \cos x \le 1\), so reject both
No solutions. That is the answer.
check  \(\cos x\) can never reach \(4\) or \(-2\), since nothing satisfies \(-1 \le \cos x \le 1\) there, so there is nothing left to substitute back

Recap · 2 min

Both halves of today were the same move.

Neither problem today was a quadratic when you met it. Both became one, and you already knew what to do from there.

two equations, two letters  substitute the linear one in → an ordinary quadratic in \(x\)
one ugly equation  nickname the repeated piece → an ordinary quadratic in \(u\)

And both have a last step people skip. On a pair, every \(x\) owes you its \(y\). On a disguise, every \(u\) owes you its \(x\), and some \(u\)-values owe you nothing at all because they were impossible.
