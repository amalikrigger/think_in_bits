Why this matters · 2 min

Find the center of a circle when all you have is its edge.

Most real circles do not come with their center marked. Today's two facts, the chord fact and the angle in a semicircle, find it from points on the edge.

Sugar mill ruins. The old windmill towers on St. Croix are round stone walls. Mark three points \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) and join them into two chords. The center is on each chord's perpendicular bisector (dashed), so it is where the two bisectors cross.

A carpenter's square. Put the \(90^\circ\) corner of a square on the rim of a round tabletop. The two places its edges cross the rim are the ends of a diameter. Do it twice, from a different spot: the two diameters cross at the center.

Hubs. A bicycle wheel, a Ferris wheel or a satellite dish has to turn about its exact center, or it wobbles. These two facts tell you where the axle goes.

Direct instruction · 1 of 6 · the chord fact

The center of a circle lies on the perpendicular bisector of every chord.

Why. A chord joins two points on a circle (a diameter is the chord through the center). \(C\) is the center, so \(CA = r\) and \(CB = r\). That makes \(C\) the same distance from \(A\) as from \(B\). In Session 1 you saw that the points the same distance from \(A\) and \(B\) are exactly the points on the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\). So \(C\) is on it.

Two ways Cambridge uses it. (1) The line from the center to the midpoint \(M\) of a chord is perpendicular to the chord, so their gradients multiply to \(-1\). (2) Draw the perpendicular bisectors of two different chords: they cross at the center.

Direct instruction · 4 of 6 · semicircle

Join the ends of a diameter to any other point on the circle: the angle there is \(90^\circ\).

\(x^2 + y^2 = 25\): center \((0, 0)\), radius 5. \(A(-5, 0)\) and \(B(5, 0)\) are the ends of a diameter. \(P(3, 4)\) is on the circle because \(3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25\)
gradient \(AP\): \(\tfrac{4 - 0}{3 - (-5)} = \tfrac{4}{8} = \tfrac{1}{2}\);  gradient \(BP\): \(\tfrac{4 - 0}{3 - 5} = \tfrac{4}{-2} = -2\)
\(\tfrac{1}{2} \times (-2) = -1\), so \(AP \perp BP\): the angle at \(P\) is \(90^\circ\)

Now drag \(P\) to the other points on the circle. The product is \(-1\) every time. Next slide: a second check with Pythagoras, and why it has to work.

Direct instruction · 5 of 6 · why it works for every point

A second check with Pythagoras, and why every point on the circle works.

Pythagoras, used backwards: if the two shorter sides of a triangle, squared and added, equal the longest side squared, then the angle between the two shorter sides is \(90^\circ\).

distance squared (Session 1, without the square root): \(d^2 = (x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2\)
\(AP^2\), from \(A(-5, 0)\) to \(P(3, 4)\). Change in \(x\): \(3 - (-5) = 8\); change in \(y\): \(4 - 0 = 4\)
\(AP^2 = 8^2 + 4^2 = 64 + 16 = 80\)
\(BP^2\), from \(B(5, 0)\) to \(P(3, 4)\). Change in \(x\): \(3 - 5 = -2\); change in \(y\): \(4 - 0 = 4\)
\(BP^2 = (-2)^2 + 4^2 = 4 + 16 = 20\)
\(AB^2\), from \(A(-5, 0)\) to \(B(5, 0)\). Change in \(x\): \(5 - (-5) = 10\); change in \(y\): \(0\);  \(AB^2 = 10^2 = 100\)
\(AP^2 + BP^2 = 80 + 20 = 100 = AB^2\), so the angle at \(P\) is \(90^\circ\)

Does it work for every point? Yes: every point on the circle except \(A\) and \(B\) themselves. Here is why.

\(C\) is the center, so \(CA\), \(CP\) and \(CB\) are all radii: all the same length.

Triangle \(ACP\) has two equal sides (\(CA = CP\)), so its two base angles are equal. Call them \(\alpha\). Triangle \(BCP\) is the same, with angles \(\beta\).

The angle at \(P\) is \(\alpha + \beta\). The three angles of triangle \(APB\) add to \(180^\circ\): \(\alpha + \beta + (\alpha + \beta) = 180^\circ\), so \(2(\alpha + \beta) = 180^\circ\) and \(\alpha + \beta = 90^\circ\).

Direct instruction · 6 of 6 · the Cambridge move: use it backwards

A right angle with its corner on a circle means the other two points are the ends of a diameter.

\(P(-3, 1)\), \(Q(1, 5)\) and \(R(5, 1)\) lie on a circle. Show that \(PR\) is a diameter, and find the equation of the circle.

\(PQ\), \(QR\)   diameter \(PR\)

Why this helps: once we know a diameter, its midpoint is the center and half its length is the radius. That is everything the circle's equation needs.

gradient \(PQ\):  \(\dfrac{5 - 1}{1 - (-3)} = \dfrac{4}{4} = 1\)  gradient \(QR\):  \(\dfrac{1 - 5}{5 - 1} = \dfrac{-4}{4} = -1\)
product \(1 \times (-1) = -1\), so \(PQ \perp QR\): angle \(PQR = 90^\circ\)

Which pair is the diameter? The two points that are not the right-angle corner. The corner is at \(Q\), so the diameter is \(PR\), the side opposite the right angle.

center = midpoint of \(PR\):  \(\left(\tfrac{-3 + 5}{2}, \tfrac{1 + 1}{2}\right) = (1, 1)\)
radius = half of \(PR\). \(P\) and \(R\) both have \(y = 1\), so \(PR\) is flat and its length is the change in \(x\): \(5 - (-3) = 8\), so \(r = \tfrac{8}{2} = 4\)
check with \(Q\), from the center \((1, 1)\) to \(Q(1, 5)\). Change in \(x\): \(1 - 1 = 0\); change in \(y\): \(5 - 1 = 4\);  \(\sqrt{0^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{16} = 4\) ✓
center \((1, 1)\), \(r^2 = 4^2 = 16\):  \((x - 1)^2 + (y - 1)^2 = 16\)

Find the equation of circle \(Q\) and hence verify that the \(y\)-coordinates of both points where the two circles meet are 11.

circle \(P\)   tangent at \(A\)   circle \(Q\)   \(y = 11\)

From the last slide: circle \(P\) is \(x^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 100\) and the tangent is \(y = -\tfrac{3}{4}x + \tfrac{29}{2}\). Circle \(Q\)'s center is where the tangent meets the \(y\)-axis; its radius is \(\tfrac{5}{2}\sqrt{5}\).

Find the coordinates of the points where the tangent meets circle \(Q\), giving the answers in surd form.

The question does not name the two points; we call them \(D\) and \(E\).

Surd form means exact, with the square root left in and simplified: \(\sqrt{20} = \sqrt{4 \times 5} = \sqrt{4}\sqrt{5} = 2\sqrt{5}\). A decimal like 4.47 is not surd form.

June 2023, Paper 11, Q12(d) · 2 of 2 · solving

Solve \(x^2 + \left(-\tfrac{3}{4}x\right)^2 = \tfrac{125}{4}\), then find each \(y\) from the tangent.

square the bracket (a negative squared is positive): \(\left(-\tfrac{3}{4}x\right)^2 = \tfrac{9}{16}x^2\)
add, writing \(x^2\) as \(\tfrac{16}{16}x^2\): \(\tfrac{16}{16}x^2 + \tfrac{9}{16}x^2 = \tfrac{25}{16}x^2\),  so \(\tfrac{25}{16}x^2 = \tfrac{125}{4}\)
multiply both sides by \(\tfrac{16}{25}\) to leave \(x^2\) on its own: \(x^2 = \tfrac{125}{4} \times \tfrac{16}{25} = \tfrac{2000}{100} = 20\)
A1 · square root; both signs, because \(\sqrt{20}\) and \(-\sqrt{20}\) both square to 20: \(x = \pm\sqrt{20} = \pm 2\sqrt{5}\)
\(D\): put \(x = 2\sqrt{5}\) in the tangent: \(y = -\tfrac{3}{4}(2\sqrt{5}) + \tfrac{29}{2} = -\tfrac{6}{4}\sqrt{5} + \tfrac{29}{2} = \tfrac{29}{2} - \tfrac{3}{2}\sqrt{5}\)
\(E\): put \(x = -2\sqrt{5}\): \(y = -\tfrac{3}{4}(-2\sqrt{5}) + \tfrac{29}{2} = \tfrac{29}{2} + \tfrac{3}{2}\sqrt{5}\)

Recap and homework · 3 min

Two facts, and a checklist for Test 1.

Chord fact: the center is on the perpendicular bisector of every chord. Center to midpoint is perpendicular to the chord: gradients multiply to \(-1\).

Angle in a semicircle: a diameter makes a \(90^\circ\) angle at any other point on the circle. Backwards: a right angle on the circle means the hypotenuse is a diameter, so its midpoint is the center.

The circle \((x - 6)^2 + (y + a)^2 = 18\) has the line \(y = 2a - x\) as a tangent. Find the two possible values of the constant \(a\).

\(a = 0\): circle and line   \(a = 4\): line (white circle)

Both values work: each line just touches its own circle. This is the answer we are about to find.

What is \(a\)? A constant: one fixed number we do not know yet. Each value of \(a\) gives a different circle and a different line.

The circle has center \((6, -a)\) (the signs flip, Session 2) and radius \(\sqrt{18}\). The line \(y = 2a - x\) has gradient \(-1\) and crosses the \(y\)-axis at \(2a\).

The question: for which values of \(a\) does the line just touch the circle (a tangent: exactly one meeting point)?

Why substitute first? Meeting points make both equations true at once, so we put the line into the circle, as on slide 6. That gives a quadratic in \(x\) whose roots are the \(x\)-coordinates of the meeting points. A tangent meets once, so there is one repeated root: \(b^2 - 4ac = 0\) (Session 3). That gives an equation we can solve for \(a\).

What the examiners saw: this route worked best. Marks were lost expanding brackets, and some forgot the 18 on the right-hand side.
